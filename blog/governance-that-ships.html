Governance that ships things, instead of blocking them

In regulated industries, "governance" is often the word teams use to explain why a system has been stuck in review for six months. That's usually a sign the governance process was designed after the fact, bolted onto a system that wasn't built with it in mind.

Design review criteria into the system, not around it

When risk and compliance requirements are treated as a checklist applied at the end, every requirement becomes a potential redesign. When they're built into the architecture from the Design phase — audit logging, explainability hooks, human-in-the-loop overrides — review becomes a confirmation step, not a discovery process.

Separate "monitoring" from "approval"

A lot of friction comes from asking one review board to both approve a system before launch and monitor it forever after. Splitting these into a pre-launch approval gate and a lightweight, metric-based monitoring process lets both move faster: approval focuses on whether the system is safe to try, and monitoring catches problems early without re-litigating the whole design each time.

Give reviewers evidence, not intentions

Review boards move faster when they're shown real evaluation results against a representative dataset, not a description of what the model is intended to do. We build evaluation suites in parallel with the system itself, so there's always concrete evidence ready by the time a review is scheduled.

The result

None of this removes the need for genuine oversight — it just moves the friction earlier, where it's cheap to resolve, instead of leaving it for the end, where it's expensive and often fatal to the project.
