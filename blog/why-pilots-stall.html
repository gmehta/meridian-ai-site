Why most AI pilots never reach production

The common explanation for stalled AI projects is that the technology wasn't ready. In our experience, that's rarely the real reason. Most pilots we're asked to look at work reasonably well in a demo. What kills them is everything around the model: who's accountable once it's live, how anyone will know if it stops working, and whether the economics were ever actually checked.

1. Nobody owns the outcome after launch

A pilot is often sponsored by whoever championed the idea, not by whoever will run the system day to day. When the pilot ends, that sponsor moves on to the next initiative, and the system has no clear owner to push it into production, budget for its infrastructure, or answer for its results.

2. The business case was never pressure-tested

Teams frequently start building before anyone has written down what "success" is worth in dollars, and what it would cost to sustain the system at scale. Without that number, there's no way to defend the investment when budgets tighten — and budgets always tighten eventually.

3. There's no plan for monitoring or drift

A model that performs well in a controlled pilot can degrade quietly once it meets messier real-world data. Without monitoring and a plan for retraining or intervention, small performance drops turn into lost trust, and lost trust ends programs.

What we do differently

Before we prototype anything, we push clients to name an owner, write down the economic case, and agree on how the system will be monitored once it ships. It's not exciting work, but it's the difference between a pilot that becomes a case study and one that becomes a write-off.
